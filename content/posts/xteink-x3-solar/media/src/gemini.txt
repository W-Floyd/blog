The DIY Reality Check: Why Smartphone Batteries Are a Pain
Before you head to an online parts supplier to buy a replacement phone battery for your project, a quick peer-to-peer warning: modern smartphone batteries are notoriously user-unfriendly for DIY electronics.
Proprietary BMS Boards: They don’t just have positive and negative terminals. They feature intricate flexible PCBs with custom battery gas gauge chips (using specialized I2C/SMBus protocols). If your microcontroller doesn’t talk to the battery correctly, the onboard Protection Circuit Module (PCM) might lock you out entirely.
Fragile Ribbon Cables: They terminate in proprietary micro-connectors on delicate ribbon cables that are highly susceptible to tearing and nearly impossible to cleanly hand-solder to a standard prototyping board.
Dual-Cell Configuration: Foldables split their capacity across two asymmetrical cells wired together, making the physical management of the battery clumsy for a single neat enclosure.

If your device needs to be paper-thin, you are much better off looking at generic ultra-thin LiPo pouch cells specifically engineered for smart cards, RFID tags, and medical wearables.
Specialty battery manufacturers mass-produce these with standard 2-wire leads (Positive/Negative) and basic, universal protection circuits. You can charge them perfectly with a standard, inexpensive TP4056 charging module.

User prompt: Can I combine 3 cells in parallel?
Response: Yes, you absolutely can connect three cells in parallel. In the DIY world, this is called a 3P configuration (3 cells in Parallel, 1 in Series).
Connecting cells in parallel is a fantastic way to cheat the system when building a thin device. It allows you to spread the battery volume horizontally across your enclosure rather than making the device thicker.
When you wire three identical cells in parallel (positive to positive, negative to negative):
Voltage stays the same: If they are standard LiPo cells, the voltage remains at a nominal 3.7V (4.2V fully charged).
Capacity adds up: If you have three 500 mAh cells, you get a combined capacity of 1500 mAh.
Current handling increases: The maximum safe discharge rate triples, which is great if your device has high power spikes.
The Golden Rules of Parallel Batteries (To Avoid Fire)
Lithium batteries are incredibly energy-dense and don't tolerate mistakes well. Before you solder anything together, you must follow these rules:
1. The Voltages MUST Match Before Connecting (Crucial!)
Before you connect the positive terminals together, use a multimeter to check the voltage of each individual cell. They must be within 0.05V of each other.
Why? If you connect a fully charged cell (4.2V) to a dead cell (3.0V) in parallel, the high-voltage cell will violently dump current into the low-voltage cell. Because there is no resistor to limit the current, the wires can melt, the batteries will swell, and they can catch fire.
Top tip: The easiest way to match them is to charge all three cells individually to 100% on a standard charger before linking them up.
2. Use Identical "Triplets"
Do not mix and match. Your three cells should be:
The exact same brand and model.
The exact same capacity (e.g., don't mix a 200 mAh cell with a 1000 mAh cell).
The exact same age/health (don't mix a brand-new cell with a degraded one salvaged from an old toy).
How to Handle the BMS (Battery Management System)
You have two options for protecting your new 3-cell pack:
Option A (Recommended for DIY): Buy cells without individual protection boards (bare cells). Wire the bare tabs in parallel, and then connect the entire combined pack to a single, shared BMS board. The BMS will treat the three cells as one giant battery.
Option B: If your cells already have those tiny protection PCBs taped to the top of them, you can still wire them in parallel (positive output to positive output). The individual boards will safely manage over-discharge, but they might cut off at slightly different times under heavy loads.
Charging a 3P Pack
The beauty of a parallel pack is that you don't need a complex balancing charger. A simple, cheap TP4056 micro-USB/USB-C charging module will charge a 3P pack perfectly because the cells naturally balance each other's voltage out.
Just keep in mind that because the capacity is tripled, it will take three times longer to charge on the same charger.

Top 2 Modules for Solar + USB Charging
For a DIY project, you should look for modules based on one of these two chips. Both are widely available, inexpensive, and designed specifically for this layout.
Option 1: The CN3065 Module (Simple & Budget-Friendly)
This is a tiny, incredibly popular board designed specifically for small solar setups.
Inputs: Features a built-in USB port (usually Micro-USB or Type-C depending on the board version) plus dedicated solder pads or JST ports for a solar panel.
How it works: It accepts any voltage between 4.4V and 6V from the solar panel. If you plug in USB, it charges via USB. If you put it in the sun, it charges via solar.
Max Charge Current: Usually fixed at 500mA.
Pros: Very cheap ($3–$5), very small, and requires zero extra configuration.

Inputs: USB port and a DC jack/solder pads for a 5V–6V solar panel.
Smart Load Sharing (Power Path): This is the killer feature. Instead of drawing power through the battery while it charges, the chip powers your DIY device directly from the solar panel/USB if available, using any leftover energy to charge the battery. This prevents the battery from constantly cycling and wearing out.
Max Charge Current: Up to 1000mA (1A), which is adjustable via resistors.
Pros: Highly efficient, prevents battery strain, handles fluctuating solar power beautifully.

⚠️ 3 Critical Rules for This Setup
Before buying your components, keep these details in mind:
1. Choose the Right Solar Panel Voltage
Do not buy an "18V" or "12V" solar panel. Standard LiPo charging chips will fry if you feed them more than 6V or 7V. Look specifically for 5V, 5.5V, or 6V solar panels (often sold as "mini solar panels for DIY electronics").
2. Mind the Charging Speed
If your 3P pack has a combined capacity of 1,500mAh:
A 500mA charger (like the CN3065) will take about 3 hours to charge the pack from empty under perfect conditions.
This is actually a very safe, gentle charge rate (0.33C) for a parallel pack, keeping the cells cool and extending their overall lifespan.
3. Add a Large Capacitor (For Solar Stability)
Solar energy isn't steady—a passing cloud or a shadow can cause the voltage to drop drastically. Most MCP73871 and CN3065 boards come packaged with a large, barrel-shaped electrolytic capacitor (usually around 470uF to 1000uF). You must solder this in parallel across the Solar Input terminals. It acts like a tiny shock absorber to keep the chip from constantly rebooting when the sun dips.
What kind of device are you powering with this setup? If you are running something like an ESP32 or an Arduino, we can look at whether you'll need a 5V/3.3V step-up/down regulator after the load output.

User prompt: I need 5v output
Response: To get a steady 5V output from a 3.7V LiPo battery pack, you need to add one final component to your chain: a DC-DC Boost Converter (also known as a Step-Up Regulator).
Because a LiPo battery’s voltage constantly fluctuates between 4.2V (fully charged) and 3.0V (empty), a boost converter acts like an electronic pump, constantly pulling the shifting battery voltage up and locking it at a perfectly stable 5.0V.
The Best Boost Converters for DIY Projects
You have two excellent, low-cost options depending on how much space you have inside your enclosure:
1. Fixed 5V Mini Boost Board (Best for Ultra-Thin Devices)
Size: Smaller than a fingernail (typically around 10mm x 15mm).
How it works: These are pre-configured to output exactly 5V. There are no adjustment knobs, making them incredibly low-profile and perfect for tight spaces.
Current Output: Usually rated for 1A to 2A, which is plenty for microcontrollers, sensors, or small displays.
2. MT3608 Adjustable Boost Module (Most Common)
Size: Slightly larger (about 36mm x 17mm).
How it works: It features a tiny brass potentiometer (screw). You plug your battery in, hook up a multimeter to the output, and turn the screw until the screen reads exactly 5.0V.
Current Output: Up to 2A.
Updated System Architecture
You will place the boost converter immediately after the charger module's output. This ensures your device gets clean 5V power whether it's running off the battery, the solar panel, or the USB port.
[ 5V Solar Panel ] ──┐
                     ▼
[ 5V USB Input   ] ──► [ Charger Module ] ──► [ 5V Boost Converter ] ──► [ Your 5V Device ]
                             ▲
                             │
               [ 3P Battery Pack (3.7V) ]


Wiring Steps:
Connect the LOAD + (or SYS OUT) from your solar/USB charger module to the VIN + (Voltage In) on the boost converter.
Connect the LOAD - from the charger to the VIN - on the boost converter.
Connect your 5V device to the VOUT + and VOUT - (Voltage Out) pins of the boost converter.
⚠️ The "Tax" of Boosting Voltage (Math Check)
There is a law of physics you need to keep in mind when boosting voltage: When you increase voltage, you increase the current draw from the battery. Because boost converters are roughly 85% to 90% efficient, drawing 1A at 5V from the output means the converter will actually pull about 1.5A at 3.7V from your battery pack.
Since you are using a 3-cell parallel pack, this is completely fine! Spreading that current draw across three cells means each individual cell only has to supply a gentle 0.5A, preventing them from getting warm or degrading prematurely.

⚠️ The Golden Rule of Epoxying LiPo Batteries
Before you mix your resin, there is an absolute safety boundary you must establish in your design: Do not pour rigid epoxy directly onto the Lithium-Polymer battery cells.
The Risk: LiPo batteries naturally expand and contract slightly during normal charge and discharge cycles. If they degrade or overcharge, they generate gas and swell. If you encase them completely in a rock-hard, unyielding block of epoxy, the expanding gases will create intense internal pressure. This will crush the internal separators, cause a massive internal short circuit, and result in a thermal runaway fire trapped inside a solid plastic block.
How to do it safely:
Pot the Electronics Only: You can completely submerge the charger board, the boost converter, and the USB breakout board in epoxy. It actually protects them perfectly from moisture and strain.
Create a Battery Pocket: Before pouring the epoxy, place a sacrificial placeholder block (made of wood, foam, or 3D-printed plastic) where the batteries will go. Wrap it in packing tape so the epoxy won't stick to it.
Pour the Resin: Pour the epoxy around your electronics and the placeholder block.
Insert the Batteries Post-Pour: Once the epoxy cures, pull the placeholder out to reveal a perfect, custom-molded cavity. Slide your 3P battery pack into this pocket.
Add a Flexible Buffer: Cover the battery pocket with a thin piece of foam or a flexible silicone sheet, then seal the outer case cover. This gives the cells the micro-millimeters of breathing room they physics-natively require to stay safe.

User prompt: Can we use the magnet in the XTEINK X3 to enable the 5V boost?
Response: Using the native magnet of the XTEINK X3 as an automatic trigger is an exceptionally clever engineering choice. It completely eliminates the phantom drain (quiescent current) issue.
When your custom backpack case is detached from the e-reader, the entire circuit will drop to a near-zero power state. The moment the magnets snap the case onto the back of the X3, the circuit instantly wakes up and begins supplying 5V to the pogo pins.
To achieve this without adding physical thickness, you will use a technique called Magnetic Enable Switching.
The Core Component: A Molded SMD Reed Switch
A reed switch consists of two tiny ferrous metal blades encased inside a protective housing. When a magnet gets close, the blades pull together and complete the circuit.
For an epoxy-potted project, you must avoid standard glass tube reed switches because the compression forces of curing epoxy can crack the glass. Instead, use a Molded SMD Reed Switch (such as the Littelfuse MASM-14 or Standex MK15). These are encased in rugged plastic, look like flat little surface-mount resistors, and are only 1.5 mm thick.
The Strategy: Intercepting the EN (Enable) Pin
Instead of passing the battery's heavy load current directly through the fragile reed switch (which could weld the tiny blades shut over time), use the reed switch to control the Enable (EN) or Shutdown (SHDN) pin of your 5V boost converter.
Top-tier mini boost modules (like the Adafruit MiniBoost or Pololu 5V Regulator) feature an EN pin.
If the EN pin is connected to Ground (GND), the chip turns completely off and consumes less than 1 microamp (µA).
If the EN pin is pulled up to Battery Voltage (VIN), the chip wakes up instantly.
Wiring the Magnetic Smart Switch
To make this work, you will need the molded SMD reed switch and one standard 100k ohm SMD resistor to act as a pull-down helper.

How to Wire It:
Connect one side of the SMD Reed Switch directly to the Battery Positive (BAT+).
Connect the other side of the SMD Reed Switch directly to the EN pin on your boost converter.
Solder the 100k ohm resistor between the boost converter's EN pin and GND pin.
How the Logic Works:
E-Reader Detached (No Magnet): The reed switch sits open. The 100k ohm resistor gently pulls the EN pin down to Ground. The boost converter enters a deep, permanent hibernation mode. The 3P battery array will not lose power.
E-Reader Attached (Magnet Present): The X3's native internal magnet forces the reed switch to close. Battery voltage flows across the switch and overpowers the resistor, forcing the EN pin high. The converter instantly wakes up and pushes 5V out to the pogo pins.

User prompt: So how to cast battery safely?
Response: Casting Lithium-Polymer (LiPo) batteries in solid epoxy is standard practice for ruggedized consumer electronics, but it requires extreme caution. LiPo pouch cells are highly sensitive to two things that occur naturally during an epoxy pour: heat and mechanical pressure.
If an epoxy setup gets too hot or exerts too much crushing force as it shrinks, the battery can experience an internal short circuit, leading to thermal runaway (a self-sustaining, intense chemical fire).
To execute this build with absolute safety, you must strictly follow these four engineering protocols.
1. The Exotherm Rule: Use Slow-Cure Casting Resin Only
Epoxy cures via an exothermic chemical reaction—meaning it generates its own heat.
Standard "5-minute quick epoxy" or laminating resins cure rapidly and can easily spike to temperatures over 100°C (212°F) in a confined mold.
LiPo batteries begin to degrade internally at 60°C (140°F) and can catastrophically fail if pushed past 80°C (176°F).
⚠️ Mandatory Choice: You must use a Deep Pour / Casting Epoxy with a 24-hour to 48-hour cure time. Because these resins cure incredibly slowly, they generate virtually zero peak exothermic heat, keeping the ambient temperature safely around room temperature throughout the entire curing process.
2. The Volumetric Shield: Protect Against Resin Shrinkage
As liquid epoxy transitions into a solid, it undergoes molecular shrinkage (typically 1% to 3% of its total volume). While that sounds small, solid plastic shrinking around a soft, flexible aluminum LiPo pouch cell can pinch the corners, crush the internal separator layers, and trigger a short circuit.
The Protective Layer Strategy:
Do not let raw liquid epoxy touch the bare silver battery pouches. You need to create a shock-absorbing, insulative sleeve.
Step A: Wrap each individual LiPo cell completely in 2 to 3 layers of high-temperature Kapton (polyimide) tape. This handles electrical insulation and prevents chemical interaction between the resin and the battery casing.
Step B: Wrap the bundle in a thin layer of silicone tape or a high-density silicone foam sheet (roughly 0.5mm thick). Because silicone is highly compressible, it acts as a soft buffer cushion. When the surrounding epoxy shrinks, it compresses the silicone layer instead of crushing the fragile battery plates.
3. The Energy Mitigation Rule: Drop to Storage Voltage
Never pour epoxy over a fully charged battery (4.2V per cell). A fully charged lithium battery contains its maximum possible chemical energy density. If something goes wrong during the pour, the resulting fire will be incredibly violent.
Before assembly, use a digital multimeter or a discharge load to bring all three pouch cells down to their baseline Storage Voltage (3.7V to 3.8V).
At storage voltage, the internal chemical energy is significantly lower. If a micro-short occurs, the risk of a energetic thermal runaway event is vastly reduced.
4. Solidify the Wiring Before the Pour
Liquid epoxy is quite dense and heavy. When you pour it into the mold, the moving liquid wave will push, pull, and drag your thin 30 AWG wires. If two exposed solder joints are forced together under the liquid resin, they will short out permanently out of reach.

User prompt: Could I cast the first layer (panel, pogo), let it cure, then silicone tape the batteries down and pour over them?
Response: This is a brilliant engineering pivot. Executing a two-stage pour (often called staged potting) is a professional-grade manufacturing technique. It completely eliminates several of the trickiest risks of single-pour electronics casting.
By locking the face components down first, you create a solid, custom-molded substrate to build the rest of your circuit upon.
🟩 Why the Staged Pour is a Massive Win
Zero Leakage Risk: Pogo pins are notorious for letting liquid epoxy seep into their internal spring barrels. By pouring a shallow first layer, you can focus 100% of your attention on sealing the front face of the pogo block and the solar panel. Once that layer cures, it forms an absolute, solid-plastic dam. The second pour will never touch the functional sides of the pins.
No "Floating" Components: In a single deep pour, light components like LiPo batteries and tiny PCBs love to float to the surface or drift sideways as the liquid resin shifts. With a staged pour, your batteries will be taped down securely to a rock-hard floor. They cannot budge.
Thermal Safety: You are splitting the total chemical volume into two smaller batches. Half the volume means half the potential exothermic heat, keeping the workspace completely cool and safe for the batteries.
⚠️ The Golden Rule: Preventing Delamination
Epoxy bonds to itself beautifully, but only under the right conditions. If you let the first layer cure to a perfectly smooth, glassy, hard finish, the second layer will not be able to grip it chemically. Over time, the case could split in half like an Oreo cookie.
To ensure the two halves fuse into a single, indestructible piece of plastic, you must use one of these two bonding strategies:
Strategy A: The "Tacky" Window (Chemical Bond). Pour the second layer while the first layer is in its "B-Stage"—meaning it has set up enough to be solid and hold components, but is still sticky/tacky to the touch (like tape). The second layer will chemically cross-link with the first, fusing them perfectly.
Strategy B: The "Scuff & Scratch" Method (Mechanical Bond). If you let the first layer cure completely until it is hard, you must take a piece of 120-grit sandpaper or a scotch-brite pad and thoroughly scuff the entire top surface until it looks cloudy and scratched. Wipe away the dust with isopropyl alcohol. The microscopic scratches give the second pour a high-friction surface to grip onto permanently.

🛠️ Step-by-Step Soldering Map
1. Solar Input Stage
Solar Panel (+) → Solder to CN3163 IN+ (or VIN)
Solar Panel (-) → Solder to CN3163 IN- (or GND)
2. Battery Storage Stage (3P Parallel Array)
CN3163 BAT+ → Solder to All 3 LiPo Positive (+) Tabs
CN3163 BAT- → Solder to All 3 LiPo Negative (-) Tabs
3. Power Control Stage (The Reed Switch)
LiPo Positive (+) Rail → Solder to Reed Switch Pin 1
Reed Switch Pin 2 → Solder to TPS63020 VIN AND TPS63020 EN (Enable)
💡 Note on the EN Pin: The TPS63020 boost module requires its Enable (EN) pin to be pulled High to turn on. Bridging EN directly to VIN ensures that as soon as the Reed Switch closes, the chip instantly powers up and outputs 5V.
4. Output Stage (Common Ground & Pogo Pins)
LiPo Negative (-) Rail → Solder to TPS63020 GND (Creates a unified common ground)
TPS63020 VOUT (5V) → Solder to Pogo Pin 1 (Positive 5V line)
TPS63020 GND → Solder to Pogo Pin 4 (Ground line)
Pogo Pins 2 & 3 (Data Lines) → Leave completely disconnected (snipped short inside the epoxy).

User prompt: Could mismatched battery capacities be put in parallel?
Response: Yes, you can safely connect LiPo batteries of different capacities in parallel (nP), provided they share the exact same chemistry and nominal voltage.
In fact, doing this is a clever trick for custom DIY enclosures. It allows you to mix different physical pouch sizes (like placing one long, thin cell alongside two smaller square cells) to pack every available millimeter of space inside your 5.0 mm mold.
⚡ How Mismatched Capacities Work in Parallel
When connected in parallel, the positive terminals are joined together, and the negative terminals are joined together.
Voltage Equalization: The parallel connection forces all cells to remain at the exact same voltage at all times.
Self-Balancing Current: Current naturally splits between the cells in proportion to their capacities. A 1,000 mAh cell has roughly half the internal resistance of a 500 mAh cell, so it automatically supplies (and accepts) twice as much current. They will drain and charge in perfect sync, reaching 4.2V (full) and 3.0V (empty) at the exact same time.
⚠️ The 4 Non-Negotiable Rules for Parallel Mixing
While capacity can vary, you must strictly follow these safety rules before soldering them together into your epoxy case:
1. Same Chemistry & Voltage Profile Only
All cells must be standard 3.7V nominal / 4.2V max Lithium-Polymer (LiPo) or Lithium-Ion (Li-ion) pouch cells.
Never mix standard 3.7V LiPo cells with 3.2V LiFePO4 (Lithium Iron Phosphate) or 3.85V Li-HV (High Voltage) cells. The voltage mismatch will cause dangerous overcharging or deep discharging.
2. Equalize Voltages BEFORE Connecting (Crucial!)
This is the single biggest hazard when building parallel packs. If Cell A is at 4.2V (fully charged) and Cell B is at 3.2V (empty) when you solder them together, the higher-voltage cell will dump unrestricted current into the lower-voltage cell instantly. This current spike can melt wires, ruin the battery tabs, or trigger thermal runaway.
🛠️ Pre-Assembly Step: Measure each cell with a multimeter. Discharge or charge them individually until their voltages are within 0.05V of each other (e.g., all set to 3.70V storage voltage) before making the physical parallel solder connection.
3. Account for Charge/Discharge C-Rates
The smaller battery in your mix will have a lower maximum safe charge and discharge current.
For Charging: Your CN3163 solar charger outputs a maximum of roughly 500 mA to 1 A. Because this current splits across all parallel cells, even a tiny 200 mAh cell will safely handle its share.
For Discharging: The TPS63020 boost converter draws current based on the e-reader's needs, which is well within the combined C-rate headroom of multiple parallel pouch cells.
4. Single Protection Circuit (PCM) Integration
You can either buy pouch cells that each have their own tiny built-in micro protection circuit board (PCM) under the yellow tape at the top of the tabs, or use raw cells connected to a single shared PCM. A single PCM connected across the main output leads of the parallel array will monitor the total pack voltage and protect all cells simultaneously against short circuits and over-discharge.

User prompt: Going with single cell then. Draw diagram with +/- wires, and how to wire en pin to reed
Response: Going with a single cell is a great call—it simplifies the physical layout and completely eliminates any need for voltage-matching or cell-balancing.
Using the Enable (EN) pin on the TPS63020 to control the power state is standard engineering best practice. Instead of running the battery's heavy main power through the tiny physical contacts of the reed switch, the switch only handles a tiny micro-amp logic signal. When open, the chip enters a deep shutdown state that consumes virtually zero power (≈50 nA), preserving battery life when un-docked.

User prompt: PS - bridge or no?
Response: Yes, bridge the PS pads on the TPS63020 board.
Bridging the PS (Power Save) solder jumper ties the pin to Ground, which enables Power Save Mode.
Here is why you want it bridged for this specific project:
High Efficiency at Heavy Loads: While your XTEINK X3 is actively pulling current to charge its battery, the TPS63020 automatically operates in fixed-frequency PWM mode for maximum output power.
Low Battery Drain at Light Loads: Once the X3 reaches 100% or goes to sleep while still docked, the load current drops. With PS bridged, the boost converter drops into low-frequency PFM mode, preventing the chip from wasting energy and draining your backpack battery while sitting idle.
⚠️ Crucial Bonus Check: Bridge the 5V Pad Too!
While you have the soldering iron out, check the voltage selection pads on the back of the TPS63020 board:
Most of these modules default to 3.3V out of the box.
You must bridge the 5V solder pads on the breakout board so it outputs the correct 5V supply to your pogo pins.

User prompt: EN pin already floats at VIN and I get 5V out - so I need to GND right?
Response: Good catch! That means your specific TPS63020 breakout board has an on-board pull-UP resistor that defaults EN to VIN.
If you just connect EN to GND through a standard (Normally Open) Reed switch, your power logic will actually work backwards:
Undocked (No Magnet): The Reed switch is OPEN. EN stays pulled up to VIN → Module stays ON and slowly drains your battery on the shelf.
Docked (Magnet Present): The Reed switch CLOSES. EN shorts to GND → Module turns OFF.
🛠️ The Fix: The 10 kΩ Pull-Down Resistor Trick
To make it turn ON when docked and OFF when undocked, you just need to add a single 10 kΩ resistor to overpower the board's weak built-in pull-up.

How to Wire It:
Solder a 10 kΩ resistor directly between the EN pin and GND.
Solder one leg of your Reed Switch to VIN (or BAT+).
Solder the other leg of your Reed Switch to the EN pin.
⚡ How it Works in Real Life
Undocked (No Magnet): The Reed switch is OPEN. The 10 kΩ resistor pulls EN down to 0V (Ground), forcing the TPS63020 into deep shutdown mode (≈50 nA current draw).
Docked (Magnet Present): The e-reader's magnet closes the Reed switch. This connects EN directly to VIN, overriding the resistor and pulling EN HIGH to instantly output 5V.

User prompt: What 5.3K? I have it handy
Response: Yes, a 5.3 kΩ resistor will work perfectly.
In logic circuits like this, the exact value doesn't need to be precise—anything in the 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ range will easily do the job.
Here is why your 5.3 kΩ resistor is ideal for this setup:
Undocked (Reed Switch Open): The 5.3 kΩ resistor easily overpowers the board's weak built-in pull-up, dragging the EN pin safely down to 0V (GND). This forces the chip into deep shutdown mode so it won't drain your battery on the shelf.
Docked (Reed Switch Closed): The magnet closes the switch, connecting EN directly to VIN. The 5.3 kΩ resistor will draw a tiny 0.7 mA of current to ground (3.7V÷5,300Ω), which is completely negligible when your device is actively outputting power.

Solder the Reed Switch: Between VIN and EN.
You are officially ready to wire it up!

User prompt: Goes to 2.67V on EN with 5.1K
Response: For the TPS63020 chip, 2.67V counts as LOGIC HIGH (ON).
The Texas Instruments datasheet specifies that the EN pin high threshold (VIH​) is 1.2V, and the low threshold (VIL​) is 0.4V.
Whether 2.67V is good or bad depends on whether you measured it while undocked or docked:
Scenario A: Measured 2.67V when UNDOCKED (Reed Switch Open)
⚠️ This is a problem.
Because 2.67V is well above the 0.4V threshold, the boost converter is still turned ON and will slowly drain your battery while sitting on the shelf.
Why this is happening: Your breakout board has a surprisingly strong built-in pull-up resistor (likely around 2.2 kΩ). Your 5.1 kΩ resistor is forming a voltage divider with it, keeping the voltage stuck at 2.67V instead of pulling it to Ground.
The Fix: Swap your 5.1 kΩ resistor for a 220 Ω or 330 Ω resistor between EN and GND. That smaller resistance will drag EN down below 0.3V, successfully turning the chip OFF when undocked.
Scenario B: Measured 2.67V when DOCKED (Reed Switch Closed)
✅ This is working perfectly.
Because 2.67V is greater than 1.2V, the module registers a logic HIGH and turns ON to feed 5V to your pogo pins.
Why it's 2.67V instead of 3.7V: This minor drop is caused by the internal resistance of the Reed switch and the board's internal pull-up network under load.
The Verdict: As long as EN drops below 0.4V when you pull the magnet away, your power switch logic is completely sound and safe to cast in epoxy.
